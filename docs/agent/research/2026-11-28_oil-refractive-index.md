Oil steals chroma twice: its refractive index makes pigments transparent, and white kills saturation. Lightness must
come from below (the ground) or from dilution (calcite), not from white.

## Why bright pigments darken in oil

The darkening is optical, not chemical: it comes from refractive index (RI) matching, not yellowing.

- Colour and lightness come from light scattering at the boundary between pigment particle and surrounding medium.
  The larger the RI gap, the more light bounces back.
- In a dry aqueous film (gouache, distemper, watercolour), particles sit partly in air (RI 1.0). Big gap, strong
  scattering: light, chalky colour.
- Linseed oil has RI about 1.48, close to many pigments: chalk about 1.57, ultramarine about 1.5, lakes about 1.5,
  many earths 1.6–2.0. Less scattering, so the colour turns deeper, more saturated, more transparent — it reads darker.
- Oil also fills surface roughness, cutting diffuse reflection (the wet-stone effect).
- Oil RI rises with age (towards about 1.55–1.57), so films grow more transparent over decades — hence pentimenti.
- High-RI pigments keep hiding power: titanium white about 2.7, cadmiums about 2.4–2.8, vermilion about 3.

RI values are approximate, given from general knowledge, not looked up.

## Light goes through, not around

When pigment and oil share the same RI, light crosses the particle boundary without bending or reflecting — the
particle becomes optically invisible.

- **In air:** every particle surface reflects and bends light. Light returns after a short path, little is absorbed,
  colour reads light.
- **In oil:** light passes straight through particle after particle and travels deep into the film. Along that long
  path the pigment keeps absorbing its wavelengths, so less light returns — darker, more saturated.

Analogy: crushed glass is white in air but nearly vanishes in water or oil. Chalk in oil does the same — a filler in
oil, a white in distemper.

So the pigment does not get darker; it gets transparent. The dark comes from the long absorption path plus whatever
lies beneath. Hence a bright ground is the strongest lever: it reflects the deep-travelling light back out.

## Solutions and trade-offs

| Solution | How it works | Trade-off |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Lean paint | Blot oil from tube paint or grind stiff; pigment near critical concentration, air voids return | Fragile, underbound film — upper layers only |
| Bright lean ground | Absorbent white chalk or lead ground under thin layers; light reflects back through transparent colour (Flemish logic) | Requires layered, thin method |
| Peinture à l'essence (Degas) | Drain oil, thin with solvent; matte, gouache-like | Same fragility as lean paint |
| Emulsion | Egg-oil tempera grassa, or tempera underpainting with oil over | Different handling, drying |
| Wax | Beeswax or cold-wax medium; microcrystalline surface scatters | Softer film, matte only |
| Coarser grinding | Coarse ultramarine and smalt keep colour; fine goes grey-dark | Grainier texture, less tinting strength |
| High-RI white | Titanium or zinc restores lightness | Shifts hue towards pastel; zinc brittle |
| Matte or no varnish | Keeps surface scattering; gloss varnish saturates further | Less protection, less depth |
| Switch binder locally | Casein or distemper for critical passages; acrylic darkens a little (RI about 1.49) | Mixed-technique compatibility |

Conjecture, untested: fumed silica (an industrial matting agent) in oil paint might restore some scattering at low
cost to the film. No known long-term data in artists' oil.

## Quantum reading

The transparency is classical; the colour and the light-matter interaction underneath are quantum.

- **Classical transparency:** wave optics (Maxwell, Fresnel) fully explains it. Electrons in the particle do
  re-radiate, but their waves interfere with the oil's so no direction is preferred.
- **Refractive index (QED):** arises from photons interacting with bound electrons. In Feynman's sum over paths,
  detours cancel and only the straight line survives. A matched particle gives identical path sums inside and
  outside — it drops out of the calculation.
- **Colour is quantum:** cadmium yellow from its band gap; ultramarine from the S₃⁻ radical in its crystal cage.
  Each absorption along the long path through oil is a quantum event.
- **Probabilistic fate:** each photon either returns or is absorbed, as a probability. Seen colour is the statistics
  of millions of such outcomes.
- **Limit of the analogy:** not a superposition — the particle is not both present and absent, it is erased by
  interference.

Conjecture, for the practice: a paint film as a field of sums over paths. The binder decides which paths cancel, so
which particles exist for light. Changing the binder changes what is visible without touching the matter — close to
the Cézanne line: perception set by the medium, not the object.

Reference: Richard Feynman, *QED: The Strange Theory of Light and Matter* (1985).

## Fastest and most solid fixes

A bright ground plus mildly lean paint handles most of the darkening, quickly and with near-zero archival risk.

1. **Bright, absorbent ground** — chalk/gesso panel or absorbent primer. Pulls excess oil and reflects
   deep-travelling light. Works only under thin paint; impasto cancels it.
2. **Moderate blotting** — paint on paper 15–30 minutes. Removes tube excess without underbinding. Stop before it
   turns crumbly.
3. **No gloss varnish** — matte or none.
4. **A trace of high-RI white** — a correction, not a method; it shifts hue.

Fast but fragile: peinture à l'essence, fumed silica (no long-term data). Solid but slow: egg-oil emulsion, coarse
hand-ground pigment, wax media.

Weak spot: ultramarine and lakes. Their RI is so close to oil that only the ground really rescues them.

## Whites in oil

Only pigments with RI well above oil's 1.48 still whiten in oil; the rest become transparent extenders.

| Pigment | RI (approx.) | In oil | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| PW6 titanium dioxide (rutile) | 2.7 | Strong white | Most opaque; cools and chalks tints fast |
| PW7 zinc sulfide | 2.37 | White | In tubes (Maimeri Opaque Zinc White); likely lower soap risk than zinc oxide — inference |
| PW12 zirconium oxide | 2.1–2.2 | White | Very inert; rare in artists' oil; Pem's tested tube: excellent but costly |
| PW11 antimony oxide | 2.1 | White | Historical, toxic, hard to find |
| PW1 lead white | 2.0 | Warm white | Not sold in EU/UK without restorer licence |
| PW4 zinc oxide | 2.0 | Soft, translucent white | Brittleness and delamination risk; top layers only |
| PW5 lithopone (ZnS + BaSO₄) | about 1.8 effective | Mild white | Old grades greyed in light; modern stabilised |
| Zirconium silicate (Kremer White) | about 1.9 | Translucent, greyish | Made for aqueous media |
| PW21/22 barium sulfate | 1.64 | Near-transparent | Extender; key to historical Venetian white |
| PW18 calcite | 1.49 / 1.66 | Semi-opaque veil | Birefringent; see next section |
| PW19 kaolin, PW26 talc, PW24 alumina, PW27 silica, PW20 mica | 1.45–1.58 | Transparent | Body, texture, matting, lake substrates |

Low-RI whites turn white again only with air around them: lean paint above critical pigment concentration, or aqueous
grounds such as gesso.

## Calcite and Velázquez Medium

Calcite lightens a dark transparent colour by dilution and a slight veil, not by whitening — the route that keeps
saturation.

1. **Imperfect match:** calcite is birefringent (about 1.49 and 1.66). One polarisation passes, the other scatters a
   little: a veil, never chalk.
2. **Dilution:** a dense transparent (ultramarine, phthalo, alizarin, lakes) reads dark because light is absorbed
   before returning. Spacing particles lets light reach the ground and come back. Chroma can rise.
3. **Less oil per volume:** leaner film, less saturated surface.

Possible limit, speculative: aged oil's RI climbs towards calcite's lower value, so the veil may fade over decades;
dilution stays.

Historical parallel: ceruse (lead white plus calcite), used by Rembrandt and Velázquez. Velázquez Medium is its
lead-free half. A home-made calcite-in-bodied-oil paste costs far less.

Source: [Natural Pigments — Velázquez Medium](https://upgrade-dec12.naturalpigments.com/velazquez-medium-50ml.html),
[Ceruse](https://upgrade-dec12.naturalpigments.com/ceruse-50ml.html).

## Preserving chroma like tempera and pastel

Tempera and pastel are intense because light returns before it is absorbed: air around pastel particles; lean film
over brilliant gesso in tempera. Fra Angelico adds pure, coarse pigments (natural ultramarine, vermilion, azurite),
flat fields, juxtaposed hues and gold.

### Guidelines, by priority

1. **Brilliant lean ground, kept visible** — chalk/gesso or absorbent primer.
2. **Light underpainting in tempera or casein, oil glazes over** — classic mixed technique; the bright layer does
   white's job for almost nothing.
3. **Lighten by dilution** — home-made calcite paste; Velázquez Medium as reference.
4. **Two pigments per mixture maximum, never complementaries.**
5. **Juxtapose rather than blend** — optical mixing keeps chroma.
6. **Lean paint** — blot, no added oil medium, let the ground drink.
7. **Choose pigments for chroma** — coarse ultramarine, high-RI pigments, clean lakes over a white underlayer.
8. **Lighten with lighter neighbours, not white** — cadmium lemon or PY184 for yellows, cobalt for ultramarine.
9. **Matte or no varnish.**
10. **Contrast** — neutral greys around a colour raise its apparent saturation.
